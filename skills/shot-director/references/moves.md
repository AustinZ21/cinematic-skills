# The 22 camera moves

Look-alike pairs first, then 12 basic moves (camera stays put / camera travels) and 10 advanced moves (texture, route, point of view, horizon, time). Each move: what happens on screen, variants, when to use it, what it gets confused with, and an example prompt to adapt.

## The pairs that look alike

Most camera mistakes come from two moves that look similar on screen but do something physically different.

- **Pan vs truck.** A pan turns left or right from one spot, like turning your head. A truck slides the whole camera sideways, like stepping to the side while facing forward. A pan sweeps the scene; a truck makes near things race past the frame edge while far things barely move.
- **Tilt vs pedestal.** A tilt turns up or down from one spot, like looking up at a tower. A pedestal raises or lowers the whole camera with the lens level, like standing on a box. Shoes to face: tilt. Over a desk to see the person behind it, eyes still level: pedestal.
- **Zoom vs dolly.** A zoom changes focal length; nothing moves, the image scales evenly. A dolly moves the camera, so foreground, subject and background shift against each other. Test: near things sliding past the edges means dolly; everything enlarging together means zoom.
- **Handheld vs gimbal.** Same path, different texture. Handheld keeps breathing and footsteps; a gimbal smooths them and keeps natural inertia. Either way, still say where it follows from and how far.
- **Crane vs pedestal.** A crane swings on an arm: height, distance and downward angle change together along an arc. A pedestal only goes straight up or down. Open up a big space: crane. Clear an obstacle: pedestal.
- **POV vs FPV.** POV is about whose eyes we borrow; it can be completely still. FPV is about how it flies: route, speed, height, turns.
- **Barrel roll vs Dutch angle.** A roll keeps rotating, so the horizon turns in frame. A Dutch angle tilts once and holds. Write a roll's direction, angle and end state.
- **Dolly zoom vs zoom.** In a dolly zoom the camera moves one way and the lens zooms the other, so the subject keeps its size while the background stretches or crushes. A plain zoom moves nothing; a plain dolly zooms nothing.
- **Long take vs slow motion.** A long take is about not cutting. Slow motion is about playback speed and can be cut many times. For AI, write "single continuous shot, no cuts".

## Twelve basic moves

Each move: what happens on screen, when to use it, what it gets confused with, and an example prompt to adapt.

### The camera stays in place

**1. Static shot.** Position, direction and framing hold for the whole shot. The actors, vehicles, light and weather can all move. The world moves, not the camera, so the performance carries the story.
- Variants: *static* (no movement), *locked off* (stresses that framing never changes), *tripod shot* (usually stable, but may still pan or tilt).
- Use for: dialogue, symmetry, performance, product shots, time-lapse change, and making a sudden action land harder.
- Don't confuse: a tripod isn't automatically static. If the head is turning, it's a pan.
- Example: *Eye-level medium shot, static. The navigator studies a star chart under a desk lamp. Position, framing, focal length and focus hold for the whole shot; only her hands and the flickering console light change.*

**2. Pan.** The camera turns horizontally in place. It can follow someone walking across, or sweep from one thing to another and reveal what's off-frame bit by bit.
- Variants: *reveal pan* (from the known to the hidden), *follow pan* (turning to track lateral movement), *survey pan* (sweeping a space to establish it), *360° pan*.
- Use for: showing a space, connecting two people, following lateral action, the "what's next to us?" wait.
- Always write: left or right, who it starts on, who it stops on.
- Example: *Wide shot, camera fixed in place. It starts on the empty docking bay doors and pans slowly left across the parked shuttles until it stops on a lone pilot sitting on a crate. Rotation only; the camera doesn't move forward.*

**3. Whip pan.** A pan so fast it smears into horizontal motion blur; only the start and end read. Used to snap to a reaction, or to hide a cut inside the blur.
- Variants: speaker to listener's reaction; following a thrown object or a sudden exit; hiding an edit at peak blur; repeated whips for comedy, music-video or action rhythm.
- Use for: sudden reactions, fast transitions, action and comedy timing.
- Don't confuse: "the camera moves quickly" gets you aimless shake. Write who it whips from and to.
- Example: *Two-shot, medium. The captain finishes his order and the camera whips left to the engineer's stunned face, with a brief, strong horizontal blur, then settles sharply on her.*

**4. Tilt.** The camera turns vertically in place, moving the eye along the height of a person, a building or an object, or from a detail out to the whole.
- Variants: *tilt up* (boots to face, street to rooftop), *tilt down* (face to the object in their hands), *reveal tilt* (hand over key information late), *follow tilt* (something rising, falling, someone standing up).
- Use for: height, scale, above/below relationships, reveals.
- Don't confuse: a tilt changes direction. If you want the camera to rise while staying level, that's a pedestal.
- Example: *Low-angle close-up starting on mud-caked boots. The camera tilts up slowly past a soaked flight jacket and a clenched fist, and stops on a calm close-up of her face. The camera stays in place; only its direction changes.*

**5. Zoom.** Changing the focal length makes the subject bigger or smaller. The camera doesn't approach, so the space between subject and background doesn't change the way it would in a push. It isn't camera movement, but it does make the frame move.
- Variants: *slow zoom in* (send attention into a detail), *slow zoom out* (add the surroundings), *crash zoom* (a sudden punch), *digital zoom* (a crop in post; costs quality and depth).
- Use for: suspense details, retro documentary feel, comedy emphasis, quick reactions, a deliberately "unnatural" look.
- Don't confuse: for the feeling of walking in, use a push, not a zoom.
- Example: *Static camera on a cockpit warning light. A slow, continuous zoom in until the blinking light fills a third of the frame. The camera position never changes; all the motion comes from the lens.*

**6. Rack focus.** Focus moves from one depth plane to another: the foreground blurs as the background sharpens, and attention is carried with it. It connects two spaces in one shot.
- Variants: *near to far*, *far to near*, *face to object* (to the clue in their hand or on the table), *object to reaction*.
- Use for: revealing information, linking two characters, replacing a cut, controlling viewing order in shallow focus.
- Don't confuse: the two things must be at different distances, or nothing visibly changes. Write the start and end focus.
- Example: *Shallow-focus medium shot. Focus starts on a spent power cell on the table in the foreground, then racks slowly to the witness's face behind it; the cell softens as her face sharpens. Camera and framing hold still.*

### The camera travels through space

These change the camera's real position, so foreground, subject and background produce parallax. Space feels deeper than with a zoom.

**7. Push in.** The camera really moves toward the subject, on a dolly, gimbal or steady handheld. The closer it gets, the more we notice expression and inner change: intimacy, tension, a realisation.
- Variants: *slight push* (dialogue, emotional shifts), *slow push* (smooth, noticeable), *fast push* (the moment something dawns), *wide to close-up* (state the start and end sizes).
- Use for: a decision, a discovery, tightening emotion, approaching danger.
- Don't confuse: a push moves the camera; a zoom changes the lens.
- Example: *Eye-level medium shot. The pilot reads a message on her wrist display. The camera pushes in slowly from waist-up to a close-up of her eyes, with natural background parallax and no zoom, and stops when she looks up.*

**8. Pull out.** The camera moves back from the subject and the visible space grows. It reveals the setting, or lets a person who filled the frame grow small: distance, loneliness, loss of control.
- Variants: close-up to medium (adds their action), medium to wide (others are here too), from a person indoors to outside the door (ends a sequence), out until the space swallows them.
- Use for: revealing the truth, establishing location, ending a scene, isolation and loss.
- Don't confuse: say how close to how far. "Pull out" alone gives no stopping point.
- Example: *Starting on a close-up of the survivor's face, the camera pulls back slowly, revealing rows of empty cryo-pods around her, and stops on a symmetrical wide shot of the whole bay. She stays centred; focal length doesn't change.*

**9. Truck (lateral track).** The whole camera slides left or right with its direction roughly unchanged. The foreground races past, the background moves slowly: strong parallax.
- Variants: *follow truck* (match a walking subject's speed), *side reveal* (slide past a wall, pillar or door frame to reveal what's behind), *parallel track*, *foreground wipe* (let foreground objects layer the space).
- Use for: walking, product profiles, exploring a space, building depth.
- Don't confuse: a pan stays and turns; a truck changes position.
- Example: *Wide shot. The camera trucks right, parallel to a courier on a hoverbike. She stays centred; support pylons in the foreground flick past while the distant towers drift slowly, giving clear parallax.*

**10. Pedestal.** The whole camera rises or sinks while the lens stays roughly level, as if on a lift. Not the same as tilting up or down in place.
- Variants: *rise* (up from behind something to reveal), *descend* (from high down to eye level), *vertical reveal* (use a table, wall or crowd as the foreground screen), *match eye line* (follow someone standing up or sitting down at the same eye height).
- Use for: clearing an obstacle, following someone standing, showing an object's vertical structure.
- Don't confuse: to see a building from base to top, a tilt is usually more natural. To stay level and get higher, pedestal.
- Example: *Starting low behind the galley counter, the camera rises slowly, clears the countertop and reveals the cook standing alone in the middle of the mess hall. The lens stays level, no tilt, and settles on an eye-level medium shot.*

**11. Tracking.** The camera travels through space over time, usually following a moving subject: from behind, alongside, or backing away in front.
- Variants: *follow from behind*, *lead* (camera backs away in front as they advance), *side track*, *through-space track* (across several areas).
- Use for: walk-and-talks, chases, entering a new space, presence and continuity.
- Don't confuse: "tracking" only says the camera follows. Add front, back or side, whether distance changes, and where they sit in frame.
- Example: *Over-the-shoulder medium shot. The camera follows the cadet from behind through a narrow corridor of server racks, two metres back, keeping him lower-centre in frame; it follows him round the corner without cutting and never overtakes.*

**12. Orbit.** The camera circles the subject on a full or partial arc. The subject stays centred while the background keeps changing: great for relationships, pressure, or showing an object's shape.
- Variants: *quarter orbit* (front to side), *half orbit* (the background and relationship change noticeably), *full orbit*, *orbit around two people* (strengthens the centre they form).
- Use for: standoffs, embraces, dances, product shots, hero entrances.
- Don't confuse: the bigger the arc, the harder it is to generate. Start with a quarter or half circle; identity holds better.
- Example: *Two-shot, medium. Two officers face each other in silence in the rain. The camera orbits them clockwise through half a circle at constant distance and height, keeping both centred, as the background shifts from the landing pad to neon-lit hangar doors.*

## Ten advanced moves

### Know which layer you're controlling

Advanced terms control different things. Separate them before writing:

- **Stabilisation:** how much shake the image keeps. *Handheld, shoulder rig, gimbal, smooth tracking.* Decides presence, breath, footsteps, smoothness. Write it like: "light handheld, restrained footstep bob". Limit: no random shake; the subject stays readable.
- **Route:** how exactly the camera travels. *Crane, drone, FPV, combined move.* Decides height, direction, turns, waypoints and the end. Write it like: "start half a metre up, through the door, then bank right". Limit: don't pass through objects; slow down and stop at the end.
- **Point of view:** whose eyes we borrow. *Objective, over-the-shoulder, POV, first person.* Decides body edges, eye line, whose view it is. Write it like: "first person, both hands at the bottom of frame". Limit: no full view of the hero's own face, except in a mirror.
- **Time structure:** does it happen in one go. *Long take, one-shot, staged move.* Decides cuts, triggers, handovers, continuity. Write it like: "after the second pillar, arc round to the front". Limit: no cuts, no jumps, direction stays consistent.

The layers stack. "Handheld POV long take" sets the shake, the point of view and the no-cut rule, but still says nothing about where the camera goes. Add the route, speed, start and end.

Skeleton for advanced shots:

> [point of view] + [shot size and position] + [stabilisation]
> + [camera path] + [speed and rhythm]
> + [start frame] + [end frame]
> + [time structure] + [what must not happen]

Example:

> Over-the-shoulder medium shot, light handheld. The camera follows the technician as he hurries down a crowded maintenance corridor, staying at his right shoulder about one and a half metres back. Passing crew briefly block the lens; he turns into the locker room and the camera stops on a stranger reflected in the mirror. Single continuous shot, no cuts, never overtakes him, no orbit.

### Texture: handheld and gimbal

**13. Handheld.** Held or shoulder-mounted by an operator, the image keeps slight drift, footstep bob and reaction lag. It feels like standing next to the event.
- Strength: *restrained* (slight breathing drift; the subject stays easy to read), *documentary* (the operator reframes on the fly), *violent* (obvious shake and chasing; short bursts only), *shoulder rig* (weightier, steadier than a phone).
- Use for: documentary, news, backstage, arguments, chases, someone losing control, pulling the viewer close.
- Don't confuse: "handheld" is a texture, not a direction. Add who it follows, the distance and how strong the shake is.
- Example: *Over-the-shoulder medium shot, restrained handheld. The medic hurries down a chaotic sickbay corridor; the camera follows from her left, about a metre back, with slight footstep bob and breathing drift. She stays clearly visible. No violent random shake, never overtakes her.*

**14. Gimbal follow.** A stabiliser removes footstep and body shake so the camera glides through space. It suits continuous follows and can turn and arc slightly even in tight spaces.
- Variants: *follow from behind* (into a new space with them), *lead* (backing away as they approach), *side track* (their size steady, the route visible), *swing round to the face* (from behind to the front to see their expression).
- Use for: entrances, corridors, walk-and-talks, tours, ceremonies, dances: immersive but calm.
- Don't confuse: a gimbal still has inertia. Writing "perfectly still, zero movement" kills the sense of walking. For a specific weight, add "slight inertia, not floaty like a drone".
- Example: *Eye-level medium shot, continuous gimbal follow. The quartermaster enters the hangar through a side hatch; the camera backs away in front of him at two metres. After the hatch it drifts slightly right so he settles on the left of frame, revealing the crew waiting for him. Smooth, natural inertia, not floaty, no cut.*

### Route: crane and drone

**15. Crane / jib.** The camera rides on the end of a long arm and swings around a pivot, rising from near a person to high above, or descending from high to their face, usually along a visible arc. Small rigs are jibs, big ones cranes; to the model the route matters more than the name.
- Variants: *low-to-high reveal* (rise from the subject to the whole scene), *high-to-low landing* (from the wide view down to the main character), *over the foreground* (clear a wall, crowd or set piece), *ending rise* (they stay put while the camera lifts and widens).
- Use for: openings, endings, stages, ceremonies, plazas, architecture, crowds; anywhere you go from a person to the scale of the place.
- Don't confuse: a crane changes distance too. To rise straight up, use a pedestal.
- Example: *Starting at a medium shot one metre up at the edge of the launch platform, the camera rises along a gentle arc up and back, clears the front row of ground crew, and gradually reveals the whole launch complex and the lit gantry, stopping on a high wide shot. Heavy, continuous motion; she stays lower-centre. Not a fast drone flight.*

**16. Drone / aerial.** The camera goes where ground rigs can't: overhead, side-on, rising, descending, circling, or swooping down from altitude to the subject.
- Variants: *top-down* (lens straight down; patterns, formations), *high forward flight* (along a road, river or ridge), *rising reveal* (up from behind cover to a city or landscape), *descending approach* (from the wide view down to a person), *wide orbit* (around a building, vehicle or landmark).
- Use for: geography, scale, journeys, architecture, landscapes, events, establishing shots.
- Don't confuse: "drone shot" carries no route. Add altitude, direction, speed, lens angle and target. Low and high drone shots feel completely different.
- Example: *Dawn over a coastal maglev line. The drone flies forward at a steady speed twenty metres above the track, lens tilted down about thirty degrees, following a silver train as it curves along the shore. The train stays in the lower third; altitude and distance hold. Smooth, no sudden dives.*

### Point of view: POV and FPV

**17. POV.** The camera sits at the character's eye line. Hands, feet, sleeves or the object they're holding can enter the bottom of frame and confirm the body. It's about whose eyes these are, not just a close camera.
- Variants: *walking POV* (eye line bobs with each step), *looking at the hands* (the object becomes the focus), *head-turn discovery* (from ahead to someone or something at the side), *waking POV* (blur to focus; consciousness returns).
- Use for: game feel, thrillers, experiential ads, a character finding a clue, operating gear.
- Don't confuse: over-the-shoulder is still outside the character (we see their shoulder). POV is their eyes.
- Example: *First-person POV at about 1.7 m eye height. The character slowly pushes open a dark storage locker; a gloved right hand and dark sleeve enter the bottom of frame. The view turns naturally left to a faded photo on the wall, then looks down to the access key in their palm. Restrained, slight breathing only, never shows the character's full body.*

**18. FPV.** First-person high-speed flight. We ride inside the camera, skimming the ground, buildings or terrain, banking into turns, feeling every change of speed. It succeeds or fails on the route: turn "fly somewhere cool" into a path you could draw.
- Variants: *ground-skimming sprint*, *through doors and windows* (space after space), *chase* (behind a vehicle, animal or person), *dive and pull up* (drop from height, level out just above the ground), *one-take traverse* (outside, through a building, out the other side).
- Use for: action, sport, architectural tours, music videos, game trailers, big transitions.
- Don't confuse: FPV doesn't mean full rolls. Constant rolling makes the subject and space unreadable; cap the bank angle on the first pass.
- Example: *Continuous first-person FPV flight. The camera starts half a metre above the entrance to a hydroponics farm, races forward between two rows of plant towers, banks about fifteen degrees right to follow an irrigation channel, skims under a catwalk, then climbs and slows to a stop in front of the control booth. Continuous path, no passing through objects, no full roll, no cuts.*

### Horizon and perspective

**19. Barrel roll.** The camera rotates around the axis of the lens. The subject can stay centred while the horizon and the whole frame turn around it. The angle sets the feeling: 10–20° is unease, 90° turns the world sideways, 180° flips it, 360° is dizzy showmanship.
- Variants: *small roll* (emotion starting to tip), *90° roll* (entering another space or set of rules), *180° invert* (falling, dreams, a world upside down), *full roll* (flight, action transitions, big visual moments).
- Use for: vertigo, losing control, dreams, falling, surreal transitions, flight.
- Don't confuse: a Dutch angle tilts and holds; a roll is the rotation itself. Write direction, angle and end state.
- Example: *Close-up of the pilot's face. The camera rolls slowly clockwise ninety degrees around her eyes; her size and position hold while the horizon turns from horizontal to vertical over five seconds, then stops. No forward movement, no zoom, no full 360.*

**20. Dolly zoom.** Also called the Vertigo shot. Camera movement and an opposite zoom are locked together, so the subject keeps its size while background perspective keeps changing. Two directions: *push in while zooming out* (the background seems to fall away and the space opens), *pull out while zooming in* (the background crowds toward the subject and the space closes in). Very demanding for AI: use a single centred subject, a clear deep background, and strong perspective lines like a door frame, corridor or road.
- Use for: a sudden realisation of danger, psychological shock, fear, vertigo, fate closing in, spatial wrongness.
- Don't confuse: if the subject visibly grows, it's just a push or a zoom.
- Example: *Medium shot of a crew member standing in a symmetrical ship corridor. The camera pulls back slowly while the lens zooms in continuously, holding his head-and-shoulders size and central position exactly. The corridor walls and hatch frames compress and crowd toward him in a restrained dolly zoom. Four seconds, no orbit, his pose doesn't change.*

### Time: long take and combined moves

**21. Long take.** One continuous shot with no visible cut. It can stay static or move between tracking, panning, orbiting and rising; the core is that time and space stay continuous. AI clips are short, so "single continuous shot, no cuts" is the practical wording. It needs the subject and the camera choreographed together: where they walk, when they stop, who enters, where the camera changes direction. Draw the route first, then write.
- Use for: walk-and-talks, tours, real-time tension, dance, backstage, continuous action, immersion.
- Don't confuse: "long" refers to duration without cuts, not to a long lens, and it doesn't mean slow.
- Example: *Single continuous shot, no cuts. The camera starts under the canopy outside the canteen module, follows a steward through the door, overtakes him on his right and turns into a lead; as he sets a tray on a table, it pans left to the woman waiting there and pushes in slowly on her expression. Everything continuous, consistent direction, no jumps, no sudden change of shot size.*

**22. Combined move.** Two or more camera moves in one continuous shot. There's no single name for it; the key is to stage the moves and give each stage a trigger. Common pairs: *track + orbit* (walk with them, then round to the face), *push + rack focus* (close in, then shift attention to a foreground clue), *rise + pull out* (from the relationship to the scale of the place), *truck + tilt* (past the foreground, then up the building), *FPV + roll* (a fast traverse ending in one controlled roll).
- Use for: introductions, character entrances, performances, product reveals, spatial transitions, a clear visual climax.
- Don't confuse: a combined move isn't every term in one sentence. Keep a short shot to two main moves; the clearer the order, the higher the hit rate.
- Example: *Single continuous shot. It opens on a medium shot behind the commander as she walks toward the edge of the observation deck; when she stops, the camera arcs a quarter circle from her right to her front; when she looks up, it rises back and up along a slow arc, from her face to a high wide shot of the orbital city. Three stages, one after another, never overlapping; same person and costume; no cuts.*
