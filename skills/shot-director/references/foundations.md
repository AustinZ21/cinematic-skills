# Foundations: where the viewer stands

Read before writing any shot. The three questions, blocking, the six layers of a shot and the order to write them in.

### Decide where the viewer stands

Watching a film, you feel like you're standing in the room. You're not. The director decided, second by second, what you look at, from where, and whether you get closer. In AI video you are the director, the camera operator and the person writing it all down for the model.

Answer three questions before writing any camera move:

- **What do we look at?** Who the subject is, what's happening, which piece of information should land first.
- **Where are we standing?** How big the subject is in frame, how high the camera is, from which angle.
- **How do we approach?** Stay still, turn in place, travel through the space, or shift focus.

Then add **blocking**: where the subject stands, when they move, when they turn, and how that relates to the camera. Describe only the camera and the model tends to move the subject and the camera together, and both drift.

Four questions to check a shot:

- Why does the subject move?
- Why does the camera move?
- What does the camera's movement reveal?
- Would this be better if the camera didn't move at all?

A move with a story job pulls the viewer along. A move added to "look expensive" becomes the endless forward float that gives AI video away.

### A shot has six layers

| Layer | Answers | Typical words |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Subject and action | Who does what | turns, runs, stops, looks into the lens |
| 2. Shot size | How much fits in frame | extreme wide, wide, medium, close-up, extreme close-up |
| 3. Angle and height | Where we look from | eye level, low angle, high angle, overhead, ground level |
| 4. Lens and focus | How the space looks | wide lens, long lens, shallow depth of field, deep focus, rack focus |
| 5. Camera move | How the camera travels | pan, tilt, push in, truck, tracking |
| 6. Pace and limits | How it starts and ends | slow, fast, smooth, handheld, start and end frames |

Plain-language versions of the words:

- **Shot size:** how much of the person fills the frame. A close-up is nearly all face; a wide shot shows the person and their surroundings.
- **Camera position:** where the camera is, how far from the subject, how high.
- **Eye level:** roughly at the subject's eye height, not from the floor or the ceiling.
- **Wide lens:** shows more space; near things look closer and bulge.
- **Long lens:** looks pulled closer; the background flattens and crowds in.
- **Shallow depth of field:** subject sharp, background soft.
- **Parallax:** when the camera really moves, near things move faster than far things.

Write the prompt in this order:

> [shot size] + [angle / height] + [lens / focus]
> + [subject action] + [move name]
> + [direction, speed, what it follows]
> + [start frame, end frame]
> + [stabilisation, and what stays fixed]
> + [light source and colour]
> + [aspect ratio, clip duration]

Filled in:

> Wide shot, eye level, 35mm. Dawn: a maintenance worker pushes a cart across an empty transit hall. The camera trucks right alongside him at a constant distance, starting at the hall entrance and stopping after he passes through the first shaft of sunlight. Stable frame, his size constant, no zoom.

Names for the same move vary, in any language. Spelling out the direction, the start and the end does more than any impressive-sounding term.
