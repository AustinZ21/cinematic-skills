# Cheat sheets

Choose a move by feeling or by scene task, the fastest decision path, and worked examples of one scene shot several ways.

### Choose by feeling

Start by asking how the viewer's relationship to the subject should change: closer, away, toward new information, or along with them. The same move carries different feelings depending on action, sound, light, speed and where it ends, so treat these as starting points.

| Feeling | First choice | Why | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calm, restrained, objective | Static | Time to watch the performance and composition | If the action is already busy, don't rush to move |
| Intimate, understanding, tender | Slow push in | Distance shrinks, expression matters more | Keep it small; stop on the emotional beat |
| Tense, pressured, suspicious | Slow push, slow zoom, or orbit | The frame keeps tightening, the background shifts | Pick one; don't use all three in a few seconds |
| Lonely, lost, goodbye | Slow pull out | They shrink into the space | The end must show the space surrounding them |
| Curious, waiting, suspense | Pan, tilt, rack focus | Key information arrives late | Write what's hidden first, then where it stops |
| Sudden, surprised, comic | Whip pan, fast push, crash zoom | Attention is forced over in an instant | Clear start and end; settle immediately |
| Free, together, journey | Tracking, truck | We share their speed and space | Say which side and how far |
| Awe, scale, power | Tilt up, pedestal up, orbit | Height and volume unfold | Tilt to show height, pedestal to clear cover |
| Documentary, raw, present | Restrained handheld | Like standing next to the operator | Start light; add shake only when it erupts |
| Unhurried, smooth, accompanied | Gimbal follow | One smooth shared path | Keep inertia, avoid floating |
| Immersed, discovering | POV | We see what they see | Write the eye line, head turns, hand edges |
| Freedom, speed, flight | FPV | The body skims through space | The route matters more than "cool" |
| Release, vastness | Crane, drone | Lifting off the ground, the world opens | Write the height change and the final wide |
| Vertigo, falling, losing control | Barrel roll | The horizon loses direction | Small for unease, big for transitions or falls |
| Shock, fear, closing in | Dolly zoom | The subject holds, the space lunges or recedes | Keep corridor or door-frame depth cues |
| Relentless, no escape | Long take | Real time, no cut to escape into | Route the subject first, then the camera |

"Tense" is too vague to choose from. Ask where the tension comes from: a decision being made (slow push), danger off-screen (slow pan or tilt toward it), two people tightening (small orbit), a detail suddenly mattering (rack focus or a quick zoom). Speed and size carry the feeling too: a slow 15° roll is a mind drifting; a fast full roll is a fall.

### Choose by scene task

| Scene | Default | Upgrade |
|---|---|---|
| Interview, monologue, a key line | Static | One slight push at the emotional turn |
| Two-person dialogue | Static, gentle pan | Rack focus to pass attention when they're at different depths |
| Someone walking across frame | Truck | Side track with foreground parallax to show the route |
| Someone walking into depth | Follow from behind | Lead from the front to see their reaction |
| Discovering a clue | Rack focus, pan, tilt | Fast push for a sudden find, slow push for a slow realisation |
| Establishing place and scale | Static wide, pan, tilt | Pull out when the subject brings us into the space; pedestal when something blocks the view |
| Product or object | Static, rack focus, orbit | Zoom for detail, small orbit for shape |
| Chase, dance, sport | Tracking, truck | One whip pan to a new target when direction changes |
| Hero entrance, tall building | Tilt up, pedestal up | A quarter orbit to change the background too |
| Scene ending, someone leaves | Static, pull out | Let the camera hold after they've gone |
| Backstage, news, real events | Handheld | Controlled shake; the subject always readable |
| Walk-and-talk, entrances | Gimbal follow | Write front, back or side and the distance |
| Stage, ceremony, opening a space | Crane / jib | Write the arc and the final high wide |
| Architecture, nature, geography | Drone | Write altitude, lens angle, speed and target |
| Finding a clue, operating gear | POV | Body edges and head turns that fit the character |
| Racing, chases, fly-through transitions | FPV | Limit the bank; don't pass through objects |
| Psychological shock, danger closing in | Dolly zoom | One centred subject, a deep background |
| Continuous performance, real-time tension | Long take or combined move | Two main moves at most, clear triggers |

There's rarely one right answer. Two people arguing can be static for realism or a slow push for pressure. The only test: does this move show the viewer one more layer, or make the change in the relationship clearer?

### The fastest path

When you don't want to read tables:

- The performance is strong enough on its own → **static**
- Something is changing inside the character → **push in**
- The surroundings change what the character means → **pull out**
- The new information is off-frame → **pan or tilt**
- The new information is already in frame → **rack focus**
- The character is crossing the space → **tracking or truck**
- We need to see the subject in three dimensions → **orbit**
- You just want a detail bigger → **zoom**

For advanced shots, ask in order:

1. Are we outside the character, or in their eyes?
2. Steady, slight shake, or off-balance?
3. Does the camera move on the ground, rise on a mechanical arc, or fly?
4. Must this happen in one go, or can it be cut?
5. Which two main moves, at most?
6. Where does the shot end, and what do we see there?

### Same scene, different feelings

An astronaut walks into an empty hangar and sits down. Same person, same room, same action:

- **Calm observation (static):** *Wide shot, eye level, static. She walks in from the doorway and sits on a crate in the middle of the hangar. Position, framing and focal length never change; she walks into and out of the visual centre on her own.*
- **Rising tension (slow push):** *Eye-level medium shot. After she sits, the camera pushes in slowly from medium to a close-up of her face, smooth, no zoom, and stops when a sound outside makes her look up.*
- **Loneliness (slow pull out):** *From a close-up of her face after she sits, the camera pulls back slowly, revealing rows of empty crates and the dim hangar, and stops on a symmetrical wide. She grows smaller but stays centred.*
- **Something's wrong (rack focus):** *Shallow-focus medium shot. Focus starts on her, then racks slowly to a helmet on the bench behind her, its visor light blinking. Camera and framing hold; she blurs as the helmet sharpens.*

And an advanced version. A courier runs into an office tower in a storm and finds the lobby empty:

- **Presence:** light over-the-shoulder handheld, following him through the doors, rain and footsteps giving a restrained shake.
- **Immersion:** a gimbal follows from behind, swings to his side after the doors and slowly reveals the empty lobby.
- **Let the viewer discover it:** first-person POV, pushing through the glass doors, looking at the front desk, then turning to the dead elevators.
- **The space presses in:** when he stops, one short dolly zoom compresses the lobby toward him.
- **No escape:** a single continuous long take from the street to the lobby, no cut.

When in doubt, generate a static version first. If the action and composition don't work still, no move will save them. Then add exactly one move and compare.

**A drill worth doing once:** take one scene and generate three versions with everything identical except the camera: static, slow push, slow pull out. Watch them side by side. It's the fastest way to feel how camera distance changes the viewer's relationship with a character.
