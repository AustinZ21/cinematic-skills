# Directing time and space

Beyond the move vocabulary: scale, geography, screen direction, depth staging, the three clocks, converging timelines, moves in time, picture, story, extra moves and a director's toolbox. Inspired mostly by Christopher Nolan.

A vocabulary tells the camera how to move. It doesn't tell you why a film feels big, or why two hours pass like twenty minutes. For that, the clearest teacher is Christopher Nolan. His films are built on two materials most AI video ignores: **time** and **space**. Below are lessons from him and from a few other directors, translated into things that can be written into a shot list.

### Space: make the audience feel where they are

**Scale needs a ruler.** A huge structure only feels huge next to something we know the size of. Nolan's wide shots put a person, a plane or a ship against the landscape so the eye can measure it: the Endurance as a speck beside Saturn in Interstellar, soldiers standing in lines on the beach in Dunkirk. In a prompt, always give a wide shot a size reference: "a single figure at the lower edge of frame", "a shuttle the size of a fingernail against the hull". In HOME, the small ships drifting past the mothership are what make those shots read as big.

**Establish the geography before you play in it.** Dunkirk shows us the beach, the mole and the sea early, so later we always know where the danger is coming from. Before an action beat, spend one shot on the layout: where the subject is, where the threat is, where safety is. Then every later shot can be tight, because the audience carries the map.

**Hold the line.** Keep screen direction consistent: if the ship travels left to right toward home, it travels left to right in every shot until something changes on purpose. Draw an imaginary line between two characters (or a character and their goal) and keep the camera on one side of it: the 180° rule. AI clips are generated one at a time and love to flip direction between them. Write it in: "the ship moves from frame left to frame right", "camera stays on her right side".

**Stage in depth.** Build frames in three layers: something near, the subject in the middle, the world behind. Depth is what makes parallax pay off, and what makes a still read as space. Write it as: "foreground: [object], midground: [subject], background: [world]".

**Use vertical space.** Nolan's spaces fold: Inception's city bends over itself, the tesseract in Interstellar stacks time as rooms. For us, vertical moves (tilt, pedestal, crane) and overhead angles are the cheapest way to make a space feel taller than the frame.

### Time: the material most videos waste

**Every film has three clocks.** *Story time* (how long events take in the world), *screen time* (how long we watch), and *rhythm* (how often things change). A two-minute film can cover a journey across space, or one hour, or one held breath. Decide which before you write a single shot.

**Run timelines at different speeds, and make them converge.** Dunkirk tells three stories at three scales: one week on the mole, one day at sea, one hour in the air. Cross-cut between them and they collide in the same moment. Inception nests dream levels where time runs slower the deeper you go; Interstellar's water planet costs seven years per hour. You don't need a feature to use this: cross-cutting a ship's approach (minutes) with a city waking (one morning) and landing them on the same beat is the same idea at two-minute scale.

**Give the audience a ticking clock.** Dunkirk is built around a ticking sound and a score that keeps seeming to rise. Tension is a deadline the audience can hear. In a music-led film the beat grid is the clock: lock cuts and moves to the tempo, and let shot length shrink as the film approaches its climax so the clock seems to speed up.

**Hold longer than comfortable.** The power of a reveal comes from the wait before it. Let the wide shot sit; let the camera stop a beat before the cut. Speed is only felt against stillness.

**Moves in time, not just in space.** The dictionary covers how the camera travels. Time has its own moves:

- **Slow motion:** stretch a moment the audience must not miss. Expensive emotionally; use once.
- **Speed ramp:** normal speed into slow (or the reverse) inside one shot, for impact or a hit on the beat.
- **Time-lapse / hyperlapse:** compress hours into seconds, static or moving. Great for "the world keeps turning".
- **Reverse:** Tenet builds whole sequences out of inverted motion. Even one reversed shot (smoke flowing back, debris reassembling) tells the audience the rules of time have changed.
- **Freeze:** stop time completely, while the camera keeps moving through the frozen moment if you want the full effect.

### Picture: make it feel real

**Practical beats perfect.** Nolan prefers real sets, real explosions and real aircraft, shot large-format, because the eye knows when weight and light are real. In AI video, "real" comes from physics: true parallax, motion blur, lens behaviour, grain, things that have weight and don't float. Write the physics: "heavy, slow", "motion blur on the fast pass", "slight film grain", "the container hangs still".

**Light has a source.** Every light in frame should come from somewhere the audience could point to: a window, a screen, a sun, a fire. Roger Deakins' images are often one strong source and deep shadow. "Single warm light from the hatch, the rest in shadow" beats "cinematic lighting".

**Colour can carry point of view.** Oppenheimer cuts between colour (his subjective experience) and black and white (the objective record). Memento runs one timeline in colour backwards and another in black and white forwards. Grade by meaning, not by mood: cold for the journey out, warm for home; one look for memory, another for the present.

**Compose with intent.** Symmetry and one-point perspective feel controlled, even inevitable (Kubrick's corridors). Negative space makes a subject feel alone. A silhouette against a bright source turns a person into a shape, which is often more powerful than a face.

### Story: what does the audience know right now?

**Every shot should change what the audience knows or feels.** If it doesn't, it's decoration. Before each shot ask: what do they learn here, and what are they still waiting for?

**Withhold, then reveal.** Memento tells its story backwards so we're as lost as the hero. You don't need a puzzle box: start a shot on a detail before showing the context, or show the reaction before the cause. Information delayed is information felt.

**Cut across time and space with a match.** A match cut links two shots by shape, motion or sound, so a jump in time or place feels inevitable instead of abrupt: a ring becomes a planet, a falling drop becomes a falling star. In HOME, the station's ring and the planet's ring could have been one cut.

**Let sound do the cutting.** Cut on a sound, let a sound bridge carry us into the next scene, drop to silence before an impact. Interstellar leaves some of its space exteriors silent, so the noise inside the ship hits harder. For us this means designing the cut list with the music open, not after.

### Moves the dictionary leaves out

- **Vehicle mount.** The camera is rigidly fixed to a car, cockpit or wing. The vehicle stays still in frame while the world rushes past. Nolan bolted cameras to real aircraft for Dunkirk's dogfights. Write: "camera fixed to the shuttle's hull, the shuttle locked in frame, the station sweeping past".
- **Body mount.** The camera is fixed to the actor, facing them, so their face stays locked while the world lurches behind: panic, drunkenness, exhaustion.
- **Locked-to-set rotation.** The camera is fixed to a room that rotates, so gravity seems to swing while the frame stays square, like Inception's rotating corridor. It's a barrel roll of the world instead of the camera.
- **Insert.** A tight shot of a detail (a hand on a lever, a gauge needle) that gives a scene its clock and its stakes.
- **Shot–reverse shot.** Two people facing each other, alternating angles from the same side of the line. The backbone of every conversation; get the eye lines right.

### A director's toolbox

One transferable rule from each of the people worth stealing from:

- **Christopher Nolan:** structure the film around time; let the audience feel scale and deadline.
- **Stanley Kubrick:** symmetry and one-point perspective; the camera as an inevitable, cold observer.
- **Steven Spielberg:** stage long scenes in depth so one moving shot replaces five cuts; push in on a face to show awe.
- **David Fincher:** the camera moves only when the subject moves, and exactly as much. No handheld unless it means something.
- **Alfonso Cuarón:** the long take as immersion; the audience can't look away because the film never cuts away.
- **Denis Villeneuve:** slow pace, huge scale, minimal movement; let the image breathe until it becomes overwhelming.
- **Roger Deakins:** one source of light, strong shapes, silhouettes; simplicity reads as beauty.

### Worked example: HOME in hindsight

Reviewed against these ideas, HOME's biggest wins were scale references (small ships against big structures) and a physical feel (real parallax, motion blur, grain). What it could add:

- **A second timeline.** Cross-cut the ship's journey with home waking up, and let them meet on the title beat.
- **One screen direction.** The ship always moves toward frame right, toward home, until the moment it arrives.
- **A clock.** A ticking element locked to the 125 BPM grid, and shot lengths that shrink before the reveal.
- **A held silence.** One beat of near-silence before the final pull-back, so the music's return lands.
- **One match cut.** Ring to ring, or hull curve to planet curve, to jump space without a seam.
